Write a score in the little notation below and hit play. Every pitch in it is synthesized twice over: once as the plain tone, and once as the least-squares reconstruction of that tone built from the other 87 keys. Same score, same notes on the page — two very different things coming out of the speakers.

What's actually sounding

Teal outlines are the notes the score asked for. Everything heat-coloured is a key the reconstruction is leaning on to produce them — in True voice, the two collapse onto each other. Under Human hands the heat gathers into two tight clumps, and you can watch them move around the keyboard from note to note; they are rarely anywhere near the note being played.

Each pitch is rendered once into an AudioBuffer by an OfflineAudioContext: one oscillator per surviving coefficient, its signed value written straight into that oscillator's gain, so a negative coefficient becomes the π phase flip derived in the previous post. The finished buffer is normalized to a fixed RMS of 0.16, which is also what the true tone is normalized to — so switching voices changes timbre, not loudness. Playback then just triggers the cached buffer per event with a short attack and release, which is why a chord of 87 sines costs the same at playback time as a single sine.
