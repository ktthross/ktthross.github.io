In developing the Gaussian Process, there are hyper parameters which we have selected for pedagogical reasons in the previous exercises. In general we would like to select these in a principled manner to create the best model possible.

## Setting up the problem

Let's revisit our questions. For some inputs $x_{i}$ and outputs $y_{i}$ there is a function $f$ such that $y_{i} = f(x_{i})$. We seek to infer a distribution over functions given the data that we have and then make new predictions given new inputs.

$$
p(y_{*} | x_{*}, X, y) = \int p(y_{*} | f, x_{*})p(f | X, y)df
$$

The key assumption we are making is that for points $x_{1}, ..., x_{N}$, $p(f(x_{1}), ..., f(x_{N}))$ is jointly Gaussian, and that Gaussian distribution has mean $\mu(x)$ and covariance $\Sigma(x)$.

Specifically, our choice of kernel for the covariance has hyper-parameters which needed to be set. For example, the RBF kernel has the form

$$
k(x, x') = \sigma_{f}^{2} \exp \left ( - \frac{1}{2l^{2}}(x - x')^{2} \right )
$$

which has the length scale parameter $l$ and the vertical variation parameter $\sigma_{f}$.

The question is, how do we set those hyper parameters.

## Deriving the marginal likelihood

For this process we will maximize the marginal likelihood of our fit to the observed data. We start with the probability of observing $y$ given the training data $X$ across all our different functions.

$$
p(y|X) = \int p(y|f, X)p(f|X)df
$$

From our premise of the Gaussian Process, we have already asserted the form

$$
p(f|X) = \mathcal{N}(f|0, K)
$$

For the second term $p(y \mid f,X)$, we start with our definition that values of $y$ are noisy observations of $f(x)$

$$
y_{i} = f(x_{i}) + \epsilon_{i}
$$

where

$$
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

We are assuming the noise in measurements is i.i.d. and that the function output is deterministic, so

$$
p(y_{i}|f_{i}) = \mathcal{N}(y_{i}|f_{i}, \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

We want to find the joint probability distribution across all $p(y_{i} \mid f_{i})$ which, since the errors are independent, will factorize as the product of all terms. This gives us the form of the term

$$
p(y|f) = \prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}(y_{i}|f_{i}, \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$


From this point we have established that both terms in

$$
p(y|f, X)p(f|X) = p(y, f | X)
$$

are Gaussian. We can also see that this is a representation of a conditional probability distribution. $f$ and $\epsilon$ are independent Gaussians, and any linear combination of jointly Gaussian variables is Gaussian. Let's combine $y$ and $f$ into a new vector $z = \{y, f\}$ such that we can write our joint distribution as

$$
p(z) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)
$$

Now we can find values for $\mu$ and $\Sigma$.

$$
\text{E}[z] = \text{E}[y;f] = [\text{E}[y];\text{E}[f]]
$$

and the covariance can be split into blocks

$$
\text{cov}(z, z) = \begin{matrix} \text{cov}(y, y) & \text{cov}(y, f) \\ \text{cov}(f, y) & \text{cov}(f, f) \end{matrix}
$$

We can use the fact that every marginal distribution will also be Gaussian. When we marginalize out $f$ in the integral, we select the block that does not depend on $f$.

$$
p(y) = \int p(y|f, X)p(f|X)df = p(y | X) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{y}, \text{cov}(y,y))
$$

We can now write explicit forms for $\mu_{y}$ and $\text{cov}(y, y)$.

$$
\text{cov}(y, y) = \text{E}[(y - \text{E}[y])(y - \text{E}[y])]
$$

We use a few properties to simplify our expression.

- $y = f + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon$ is Gaussian noise by construction. This means $\text{E}[\epsilon] = 0$ and consequently $\text{E}[y] = \text{E}[f]$.
- $\text{E}[\text{E}[x]] = \text{E}[x]$
- $\text{E}[A + B] = \text{E}[A] + \text{E}[B]$

The result is a covariance of $K + I \sigma_{y}^{2}$ and a mean of $\mu = \text{E}[f]$.

Since we can always center our data to the mean, there is no loss in just setting our mean to 0 now. The final expression then becomes

$$
p(y | X) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, K + I \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

Recall that our covariance matrix $K$ has some set of parameters. The covariance is defined by the kernel so we express our covariance matrix as a function of the parameter.

$$
p(y | X) \sim \mathcal{N}_{\theta}(0, K + I \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

With our closed expression and a set of parameters, we can search for the best set by taking the derivative and maximizing our likelihood, or in this case our marginal likelihood (as it's called in the field)

$$
\log p(y | X) = - \frac{1}{2} y K_{y}^{-1}y - \frac{1}{2}\log \text{det}(K_{y}) - \frac{N}{2}\log (2 \pi)
$$

Whew. Now that I have derived the equation to my satisfaction, let's talk about what everything means.

## What the terms mean

1. You could just minimize training error to choose hyperparameters. Why is the log marginal likelihood a better objective? What does the complexity penalty term buy you that training error doesn’t?

The first term is referred to as the data fit term. This is the Mahalanobis distance squared, which determines how close the vector is to the mean of the distribution. The closer the vector is to the mean, the closer this distance gets to 0.

$$
y^{T}K_{y}^{-1}y 
$$


The second term

$$
\log \text{det}(K_{y})
$$

will get large if the transformation the matrix executes strongly scales or stretches the space. The stronger the transformation, the larger the term, and the more the fit is penalized.

If we instead try to minimize the training error, the consequence is that the length scale will go to zero. To minimize the error, we want to fit exactly, which will fail to generalize and make interpolation worse. A validation set can be used to reduce overfitting, but then we lose training data. The marginal likelihood doesn't need held-out data, it's differentiable, and it has the regularization term.

2. How many extra operations do you need to compute the log marginal likelihood once $L$ and $\alpha$ are in hand?

In previous steps we used Cholesky decomposition for numerical stability and efficiency.  If we have $L$ and $\alpha$ already computed, the log of the determinant is just a sum over the log of diagonal elements of $L$. We can bring the exponent on the log as a multiplier so we don't even need to exponentiate anything. The fit term is a vector multiplication. Computing the marginal likelihood is very cheap.

3. If you evaluate the log marginal likelihood at lengthscale = 0.01 versus lengthscale = 100 on your sin(x) data, which do you expect to score higher and why?

In this question we are wondering how the RBF would fit to the sin data. As a reminder, the RBF kernel is

$$
k(x, x') = \sigma^{2}_{f} \exp \left( - \frac{\|x - x'\|^{2}}{2 l^{2}} \right)
$$

For a very small $l$, the correlations between points fall off quickly, while for large $l$ the correlations are long distance. For large $l$, the sin function has lots of variation, but the long correlation would require the model to interpret that variation as noise. For small $l$ you lose the interpolation between adjacent points, but you can capture the observed points with the variance term $\sigma$. It's likely that the best $l$ will be shorter than the period of the sin function.

Swapping in the periodic (exponentiated sine-squared) kernel with its period fixed to the true period of $\sin(x)$ gives a slightly higher best log marginal likelihood (189.4 vs 187.6). Even though the data is clearly periodic, the RBF performs basically as well as the sine kernel for short lengthscales, and this is even more apparent when the three terms are separated. It's only when the length scale goes past 3, about $\pi$, that the RBF fails to model correctly, while the sine kernel can still capture the periodicity. Even more interesting is the fact that the complexity term follows the same trend in both kernels. The RBF fails because it can't fit, but both kernels steadily get penalized for complexity.

If the two kernels are good fits at a short lengthscale despite the obvious periodicity, it shows that RBF can be a really good fit it will fail to extrapolate. When looking at the data, getting the right scale and evaluating on held-out extrapolation data is important.
