In developing the Gaussian Process, there are hyper parameters which we have selected for pedagogical reasons in the previous exercises. In general we would like to select these in a principled manner to create the best model possible.

Lets revisit our questions. For some inputs $x_{i}$ and outputs $y_{i}$ there is a function $f$ such that $y_{i} = f(x_{i})$.  We seek to infer a distribution over functions given the data that we have and then make new predictions given new inputs.

$$
p(y_{*} | x_{*}, X, y) = \int p(y_{*} | f, x_{*})p(f | X, y)df
$$

The key assumption we are making is that for points $x_{1}, ..., x_{N}$ that $p(f(x_{1},...,f(x_{N})))$ is jointly Gaussian and that Gaussian distribution has mean $\mu(x)$ and covariance $\Sigma(x)$.

Specifically, our choice of kernel for the covariance has hyper-parameters which needed to be set. For example, the RBF kernel has the form

$$
k(x, x') = \sigma_{f}^{2} \exp \left ( - \frac{1}{2l^{2}}(x - x')^{2} \right )
$$

which has the length scale parameter $l$ and the vertical variation parameter $\sigma$.

The question is, how do we set those hyper parameters.

For this process we will maximize the marginal likelihood of our fit to the observed data.  We start with the probability of observing $y$ given the training data $X$ across all our different functions.

$$
p(y|X) = \int p(y|f, X)p(f|X)df
$$

From our premise of the Gaussian Process, we have already asserted the form

$$
p(f|X) = \mathcal{N}(f|0, K)
$$

For the second term $p(y|f,X)$ we start with our definition that values of y are noisy observations of $f(x)$

$$
y_{i} = f(x_{i}) + \epsilon_{i}
$$

where

$$
\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

Where are assuming the noise in measurements is i.i.d and that the function output is deterministic so

$$
p(y_{i}|f_{i}) = \mathcal{N}(y_{i}|f_{i}, \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

We want to find the joint probability distribution across all $p(y_{i}|f_{i})$ which, since the error are independent will factorize as the product of all terms. This gives us the form of the term

$$
p(y|f) = \Pi_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{N}(y_{i}|f_{i}, \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

From this point we have established that both terms in

$$
p(y|f, X)p(f|X) = p(y, f | X)
$$

are Gaussian. We can also see that this is a representation of a conditional probability distribution. The product of two Gaussian's is also a Gaussian. Lets combine y and f into a new vecttor $z = \set{y, f}$ such that we can write our joint distribution as

$$
p(z) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)
$$

Now we can find values for $\mu$ and $\Sigma$.

$$
\text{E}[z] = \text{E}[y + f] = \text{E}[y] + \text{E}[f]
$$

and the covariance can be split into blocks

$$
\text{cov}(z, z) = \begin{matrix} \text{cov}(y, y) & \text{cov}(y, f) \\ \text{cov}(f, y) & \text{cov}(f, f) \end{matrix}
$$

We can use the fact that every marginal distribution will also be Gaussian. When we marginalize out $f$ in the integral, we select the block that does not depend on f.

$$
p(y) = \int p(y|f, X)p(f|X)df = p(y | X) \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{y}, \text{cov}(y,y))
$$

we can now write explicit forms for $\mu_{y}$ and $\text{cov}(y, y)$.

$$
\text{cov}(y, y) = \text{E}[(y - \text{E}[y])(y - \text{E}[y])]
$$

We use a few properties to simplify our expression.

- $y = f + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon$ is gaussian noise by construction. This means $E[\epsilon] = 0$ and consequently $\text{E}[y] = \text{E}[f]$.
- $\text{E}[\text{E}[x]] = \text{E}[x]$
- $\text{E}[A + B] = \text{E}[A] + \text{E}[B]$

The result is 

$K + I \sigma_{y}^{2}$ and $\mu = \text{E}[f]$

Since we can always center our data to the mean, there is no loss in just setting our mean to 0 now. The final expression then becomes

$$
p(y | X) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, K + I \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

Recall that our covariance matrix $K$ has some set of parameters. The covariance is defined by the kernel so we express our covariance matrix as a function of the parameter.

$$
p(y | X) \sim \mathcal{N}_{\theta}(0, K + I \sigma_{y}^{2})
$$

With our closed expression and a set of parameters we can search for the best set by taking the derivative and maximizing our likelihood or in this case our marginal likelihood (as its called in the field)

$$
\log p(y | X) = - \frac{1}{2} y K_{y}^{-1}y - \frac{1}{2}\log |K_{y}| - \frac{N}{2}\log (2 \pi)
$$
